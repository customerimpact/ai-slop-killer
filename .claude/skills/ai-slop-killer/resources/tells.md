# Tells: what readers notice

The scanner catches the patterns that have a fixed shape. This file is for the judgment pass: confirming or dismissing scanner hits, and finding the tells only a reader sees.

**Combinations matter more than single words.** One "robust" in a careful paragraph means nothing. Five vocabulary hits, a pivot and a stock opener in 100 words is a template. Weigh the density, not each word.

## Dismiss a scanner hit when

- **The word is literal.** A painted landscape, a server that serves as a backup, a real journey.
- **A source is named right after the vague attribution.** "Research shows (Smith, 2021)…" is fine.
- **The negation answers a belief the reader really holds.** "Most people think you need a big list to launch. You don't: our first launch went to 212 people." This is a real correction with evidence. A pivot against a claim nobody made stays flagged.
- **The writer's voice permits it.** The scanner already handles this when a profile exists.
- **It's a quotation** of someone else's words.

## Judgment tells (the scanner can't see these)

### Generic content
- **The horoscope test.** Could this paragraph appear in a piece about any company or topic? "Communication is key to strong teams" fails. Fix: the specific thing that happened, with a number, a name or a moment.
- **Fake specifics.** Numbers or names that look precise but do no work ("a 2023 study of 500 companies" with no source, "Sarah, a marketing manager" as a made-up persona). Fix: a real source, or cut it.
- **Explanation where an opinion belongs.** The piece describes what something is ("AI tools can help with writing") when the reader came for a view. Fix: the writer's stance, plainly.
- **Balanced fence-sitting.** Two sides presented to avoid a position the writer actually holds. An honest "it depends" with the conditions named stays.

### Structure
- **The forced three.** Three items where the real count is two or four, one of them padding. Check whether each item survives on its own.
- **A summary after every section,** or a closing paragraph that repeats the intro.
- **Template arc.** Hook, then a numbered list of lessons, then a question to the audience. Fine once; a tell when the content is generic.
- **Uniform paragraphs.** Every paragraph the same size and shape.
- **Signposting.** "Now that we've covered X, let's look at Y."

### Language
- **Synonym cycling.** The same thing called something new in every sentence ("the platform… the solution… the tool… the system") to avoid repetition. Humans repeat the plain word.
- **Abstract nouns doing the work of verbs.** "Drive alignment", "enable growth", "deliver value". Fix: who does what.
- **Intensifier padding.** Truly, deeply, incredibly, genuinely, really, absolutely.
- **Hedge stacks.** "May potentially help to somewhat improve." One honest hedge stays.
- **Even-handed adjective pairs.** "Clear and concise", "simple yet powerful", "fast and reliable", in every paragraph.

### Voice
- **No person in it.** No "I", no opinion, no moment, no detail only this writer could know.
- **Register whiplash.** A casual post that suddenly says "Furthermore, it is imperative".
- **Wrong channel norms.** See `channels/<channel>.md`.

## What human writing tends to have

- Uneven rhythm: a four-word sentence next to a thirty-word one.
- Contractions, plain connectors (and, but, so, still, anyway), and repeated plain words.
- First-hand detail: a date, a price, a place, a person, a mistake.
- An opinion, sometimes a wrong-sounding one, stated without a hedge stack.
- Paragraphs of different sizes.
- An ending that stops when the point is made.
