# Flow and order

**Its job:** move the reader from attention to action in an order where each part answers the question the last part raised, so nothing feels out of place or premature.

**Applies to:** all copy longer than a few lines. It matters most on sales pages, long emails, funnel sequences and webinar or VSL scripts.

## What good looks like
- The sequence follows the reader's questions: what is it, is it for me, does it work, what do I get, what does it cost, what if it doesn't work, what now.
- The price arrives after enough value and proof to make it feel fair.
- Each section ends with a line that pulls into the next one.
- Proof sits next to the claim it supports, not in a pile at the bottom.
- Objections are handled where they come up, and again near the call to action.
- The ask comes when the reader is ready, and is repeated at natural decision points.

## What usually goes wrong
- The offer or price appears before the reader knows why they'd want it.
- A long origin story or company history before the reader cares.
- Sections stacked like a template (features, testimonials, FAQ) with no link between them.
- The same point made three times in different sections while a key question is never answered.
- Jumps in logic: a claim with no setup, or a benefit that assumes the reader knows a feature.
- A single call to action at the very bottom of a long page.

## Scoring (0–3)
- **0:** the order works against the sale: price before value, key questions unanswered, or parts that contradict each other.
- **1:** present but weak: the sections are all there but feel bolted together; the reader has to jump around.
- **2:** does its job: a logical order that answers the reader's questions as they arise, with clear transitions.
- **3:** a reason to buy on its own: the piece builds momentum so each part makes the next one feel inevitable, and the ask lands as the obvious next step.

## Fixes to suggest
- List the questions a buyer asks in order, then rearrange sections to answer them in that order.
- Move the price below the value and proof, and the guarantee right next to the price.
- Add a bridge line at the end of each section that sets up the next one.
- Cut or shorten any section that repeats an earlier point.
- Add a call to action after each major persuasion peak, not only at the end.

## Example
Weak:
> Clay Weekends: €149. Buy now.
> About us: we started in a garage in 2015...
> What you'll learn: 8 modules.

Stronger:
> Never touched clay? By Sunday you'll have made your first three bowls.
> Here's how the weekend runs, module by module.
> What past students made in week one (photos).
> €149, lifetime access, and the full course is yours to keep.

The stronger one earns each step before asking for the next, so the price follows the proof.

**Facts only the writer has:** the real course or product structure, which objections buyers raise most, and where in the process they usually drop off.
