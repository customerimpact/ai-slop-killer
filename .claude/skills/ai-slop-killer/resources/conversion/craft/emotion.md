# Emotion

**Its job:** make the reader feel the problem and the relief through scenes and details, so the decision has a feeling behind it, without the copy telling them what to feel.

**Applies to:** all copy that asks for a decision. It matters most in problem sections, stories, testimonials and the close of sales pages, emails and scripts.

## What good looks like
- Feelings come from situations: a moment, a place, a time of day the reader has lived through.
- The problem is shown at the level of daily life (the Sunday evening, the late invoice, the sore back after a long call).
- The outcome is pictured just as concretely: what the reader does, sees or stops worrying about.
- Emotion is in proportion to the product. A coffee subscription isn't a life rescue.
- Stories have a real turning point and a detail that couldn't be invented casually.
- Logic and proof sit close by, so the feeling has something to stand on.

## What usually goes wrong
- Announced emotion: "imagine how amazing you'll feel", "you'll love it", "feel confident".
- Fear and shame pushed too hard, or pain described in ways that feel manipulative.
- Big feelings attached to small products, which reads as fake.
- Stories with no specific moment, just a summary of struggle and success.
- Flat, feature-only copy that gives the reader no reason to care now.
- Fake urgency used as a substitute for real desire.

## Scoring (0–3)
- **0:** no emotional pull where a decision is asked for, or the emotion is manipulative or overblown.
- **1:** present but weak: feelings are named or announced, but not shown; the reader understands but doesn't feel it.
- **2:** does its job: at least one scene for the problem and one for the outcome, in proportion and believable.
- **3:** a reason to buy on its own: the reader recognises a moment from their own life and wants the change before they reach the price.

## Fixes to suggest
- Replace every "you'll feel..." line with the moment that causes that feeling.
- Ask the writer for one real customer moment (before and after) and build a short scene from it.
- Scale the emotion to the stakes: lighter for small purchases, more depth for big ones.
- Cut shame and fear lines that blame the reader. Describe the situation, not their failure.
- Put a fact or proof point right after each emotional peak.

## Example
Weak:
> Imagine how amazing you'll feel with Clay Weekends! You'll finally feel creative and fulfilled.

Stronger:
> Saturday, 10 a.m. Your hands are grey with clay and your phone's in another room.
> By Sunday evening there's a wobbly bowl on the shelf, and you made it.

The stronger one gives the reader a weekend they can picture, so the feeling arrives without being named.

**Facts only the writer has:** real customer stories, specific moments buyers describe before and after, and photos or details from actual use.
