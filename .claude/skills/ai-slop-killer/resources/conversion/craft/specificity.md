# Specificity

**Its job:** make every claim believable by backing it with numbers, names, details and examples the reader can picture or check.

**Applies to:** all copy. It matters most in headlines, benefit lists, proof sections and offers, where vague claims get skimmed and forgotten.

## What good looks like
- Results come with a number, a timeframe and a person or situation ("cut Sunday scheduling from 3 hours to 40 minutes").
- Features are described by what they do in a moment the reader knows, not by category labels.
- Proof names who said it (first name, role, context) and what changed for them.
- Odd, exact numbers where the writer has them (€14, 12 weeks, 3 sessions a week), not round puffery.
- Concrete nouns and verbs: things you can see, actions you can film.
- Claims are scoped honestly. The copy says who it works for and under what conditions.

## What usually goes wrong
- Superlatives with nothing behind them: "best", "world-class", "powerful", "easy".
- Benefits that could fit any product in any market ("save time", "grow your business").
- Testimonials that only praise ("Amazing course!") and describe no result.
- Numbers without a reference point ("save 30%" of what, compared to what).
- Abstract nouns stacked up: solutions, experiences, outcomes, journeys.
- Invented or rounded figures that a careful reader won't trust.

## Scoring (0–3)
- **0:** the piece is made of claims with no support, or the specifics look made up.
- **1:** present but weak: a few numbers or details, but most benefits and proof stay generic.
- **2:** does its job: main claims carry a number, example or named source, and the reader can picture the result.
- **3:** a reason to buy on its own: the detail is so exact and checkable that it does the persuading, and no competitor could copy the lines unchanged.

## Fixes to suggest
- For each claim, ask "how much, how fast, for whom, compared to what?" and add the answer.
- Swap each adjective for the fact that earns it ("easy" becomes "set up in 10 minutes, no accountant needed").
- Rewrite testimonials as before-and-after: what was happening, what they did, what changed.
- Replace category words with a scene: what the reader sees on screen, on the table, in the cup.
- Cut any claim the writer can't support yet, and ask for the fact instead of guessing.

## Example
Weak:
> Harbor Roast brings you amazing, high-quality coffee with total flexibility.

Stronger:
> Beans roasted on Monday, in your letterbox by Thursday.
> From €14 a delivery. Skip a week or cancel from your account in two clicks.

The stronger one gives dates, a price and the exact cancellation step, so the reader can test every word.

**Facts only the writer has:** real results with timeframes, customer names and roles (with permission), exact prices, dates, delivery times and any measured comparisons.
