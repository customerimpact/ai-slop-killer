# Reader language

**Its job:** describe the problem, the desire and the product in the words buyers already use, so the reader feels understood before they're sold to.

**Applies to:** all copy. It matters most in headlines, problem sections, ads and subject lines, where the reader decides in a glance whether this is about them.

## What good looks like
- The problem is described the way a buyer would say it to a friend, not the way the company describes it in a meeting.
- Phrases look lifted from reviews, support tickets, sales calls or forum posts: specific, a bit messy, human.
- The reader's own objections and doubts appear in their wording ("I've tried apps before and stopped after a week").
- Product terms are translated into the job the reader is trying to get done.
- The vocabulary fits the audience's level: no jargon for beginners, no hand-holding for experts.
- Search-style phrases (how people would type the problem) show up in headings.

## What usually goes wrong
- Company language: "solutions", "platform", "ecosystem", "empower", "optimise workflows".
- The writer's expertise leaks in: technical terms the buyer doesn't use or care about.
- Pain described in polished, abstract terms that no real person says ("financial complexity").
- Feature names from the product team treated as if the reader knows them.
- A tone of talking about the reader ("users struggle with...") instead of to them.
- Borrowed buzzwords from the industry that make every competitor sound the same.

## Scoring (0–3)
- **0:** written entirely in company or industry language; the buyer wouldn't recognise their own problem.
- **1:** present but weak: a few relatable phrases, but most of the copy sounds like a brochure.
- **2:** does its job: problem and benefits use plain buyer words, and jargon is gone or explained.
- **3:** a reason to buy on its own: lines read like the reader's own thoughts, so they feel the seller knows them better than competitors do.

## Fixes to suggest
- Ask the writer for raw buyer words: reviews, survey answers, call notes, emails. Pull exact phrases from them.
- Replace each company term with the sentence a customer would use for the same thing.
- Write the problem section as the reader would complain about it, then tidy only the grammar.
- Name the objection in the reader's voice before answering it.
- Check headings against how people would search for the problem.

## Example
Weak:
> ShiftDesk optimises workforce allocation across multi-site hospitality operations.

Stronger:
> No more rebuilding the rota every time someone calls in sick.
> ShiftDesk shows every manager across your restaurants who's free, and fills the gap in a few taps.

The stronger one opens with the moment managers actually complain about, in the words they'd use.

**Facts only the writer has:** real customer quotes, review snippets, survey answers and the exact phrases buyers use on sales calls or in support tickets.
