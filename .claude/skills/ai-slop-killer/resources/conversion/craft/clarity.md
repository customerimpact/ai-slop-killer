# Clarity

**Its job:** a cold reader should be able to say what's being sold, who it's for and what happens next after a few seconds of reading.

**Applies to:** all copy. It matters most above the fold on sales and landing pages, in the first line of an ad and in the subject line and opening of a marketing email.

## What good looks like
- The first screen names the product category in words a stranger would use ("bookkeeping app", "pottery course"), not only a brand name or a slogan.
- The reader can tell who it's for: a role, a situation or a problem they'd recognise as theirs.
- The main outcome is stated plainly, once, before any clever angle.
- The next step is obvious: one action, one label, and the reader knows what they get when they click.
- Each sentence makes one point. You can read it once and repeat it back.
- Terms the reader may not know are explained the first time, or replaced.

## What usually goes wrong
- A headline that's all mood ("Work, reimagined") with no product, no reader and no result.
- Insider words, acronyms or feature names the buyer has never heard.
- The offer only becomes clear halfway down, after the story or the brand manifesto.
- Several audiences addressed at once, so nobody feels addressed.
- Long sentences carrying two or three claims, so the reader loses the main one.
- Buttons labelled "Learn more" or "Get started" with no hint of what happens next.

## Scoring (0–3)
- **0:** a cold reader can't tell what it is or who it's for, or the copy actively confuses them.
- **1:** present but weak: you can work it out, but only by reading past the first screen or guessing from images.
- **2:** does its job: product, reader and outcome are clear in the opening, and the next step is plain.
- **3:** a reason to buy on its own: so clear and so precisely aimed that the right reader recognises themselves at once and knows exactly what to do.

## Fixes to suggest
- Run the five-second test: show the first screen to someone cold and ask what it is and who it's for. Rewrite until they get both.
- Add a plain subhead under any clever headline that names the product and the reader.
- Replace internal names and jargon with the words a buyer would type into a search box.
- Split any sentence with more than one claim. Keep the main claim first.
- Rename the button after what the reader gets ("Start my 14-day trial").

## Example
Weak:
> Ledgerly. Money, made calm.
> Join the movement.

Stronger:
> Ledgerly is a bookkeeping app for freelancers.
> Snap receipts, send invoices and see what you owe in tax, in one place.
> Try it free for 14 days.

The stronger one says what it is, who it's for and what to do next, before asking for any feeling.

**Facts only the writer has:** the exact audience the product is built for, the main outcome customers report, and what happens after the click (trial length, demo, delivery).
