# Story

**Its job:** carry the reader through a real experience (the founder's, the product's origin or a customer's) so they relate, trust the seller and believe the change is possible for them.

**Needed on:** sales pages, webinar and VSL scripts and long marketing emails, often in the lead or just after the problem. Landing pages can use a short version. Ads can open with one scene. Product pages for simple goods and pages for buyers who want specs fast can skip it.

## What good looks like
- It starts in a specific moment, with a place, a time or a line of dialogue, not with a biography.
- The person in it faced the same problem as the reader and the reader can see themselves in it.
- There's a turning point: what was tried, what failed, and what was learned that led to the product.
- It's short enough to keep momentum and every scene earns its place.
- It connects directly to the offer. The reader finishes it knowing why this product exists.
- Details feel true: small, odd, specific things nobody would invent.

## What usually goes wrong
- A CV in paragraph form: degrees, years of experience, past jobs.
- A rags-to-riches arc so polished it reads as made up.
- The story is about the founder's success, not the reader's problem.
- It wanders, with scenes that don't lead to the product.
- No turning point, so it's just a list of events.
- Invented customers or composite stories presented as real.

## Scoring (0–3)
- **0:** missing where it's needed, or it actively hurts (for example a story that reads as fake).
- **1:** present but weak: biography or vague arc, hard to relate to, loosely tied to the offer.
- **2:** does its job: a specific, relatable story with a clear turning point that leads to the product.
- **3:** a reason to buy on its own: the reader sees themselves in it, believes it and feels the product was made for them.

## Fixes to suggest
- Open on the worst moment, in one scene, then backfill only what's needed.
- Cut every detail that doesn't serve the problem, the turn or the product.
- Name what was tried and why it failed. That's where the mechanism comes from.
- End the story with the sentence that introduces the product.
- If it's a customer story, get permission and use their own words.

## Example
**Weak (Harbor Roast):**
> Our founder has always been passionate about coffee. After years in the industry, he decided to share his love of great coffee with the world.

**Stronger (Harbor Roast):**
> The beans in my cupboard had a roast date eight months old. I'd paid €12 for them at the supermarket. That week I started roasting in my garage and posting bags to six friends, every two weeks, within days of roasting. Two years later that's still the whole idea.

Why it works: a concrete, slightly embarrassing detail sets up the exact promise the subscription makes.

**Facts only the writer has:** the real founder or customer story, dates, places, what was tried, permission to tell it.
