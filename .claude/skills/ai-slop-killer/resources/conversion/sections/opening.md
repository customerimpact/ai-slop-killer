# Opening

**Its job:** make the reader feel recognised in the first few lines after the headline, so they decide to keep reading.

**Needed on:** long sales pages, webinar and VSL scripts, and marketing emails, where the first 50 to 150 words carry most of the drop-off. Landing pages and product pages need a short version (one or two lines) under the subheadline or hero. Ads need it as the first sentence after the hook. Order and checkout pages can skip it.

## What good looks like
- It starts in the reader's world: a moment, a frustration or a desire they'll recognise right away.
- The first sentence is short and easy. The reader slides into the second.
- It picks up the exact thread of the headline instead of starting a new topic.
- It's specific: a time of day, a situation, a thing the reader says to themselves.
- It creates a reason to read on, such as an open question, a surprising fact or a story that hasn't resolved yet.
- It gets to the point inside a few lines. No warm-up.

## What usually goes wrong
- Throat-clearing: "Welcome!", "In this article", "We're so excited to share".
- It opens with the company's history or mission instead of the reader.
- A generic scene anyone could write ("Running a business is hard").
- It breaks the promise of the headline and starts somewhere else.
- A long, slow first paragraph full of clauses.
- A string of questions the reader is expected to answer "yes" to.

## Scoring (0–3)
- **0:** missing where it's needed, or it actively hurts.
- **1:** present but weak: generic scene-setting, about the company, or slow to get going.
- **2:** does its job: starts with the reader, follows on from the headline and gives a reason to keep reading.
- **3:** a reason to buy on its own: so precise about the reader's situation that they feel understood, and they can't stop at line three.

## Fixes to suggest
- Delete the first paragraph and see if the second one is a better start. It usually is.
- Open on one concrete moment from the reader's week, in their words.
- Cut the first sentence to under 12 words.
- Move any company background further down the page or remove it.
- End the opening with a line that pulls into the next section, such as a promise of what's coming.

## Example
Weak (Harbor Roast):
> At Harbor Roast, we're passionate about coffee and have been sourcing beans for years. We believe everyone deserves a great cup.

Stronger:
> It's 7:40, the bag is empty again, and the supermarket stuff tastes like cardboard. You meant to reorder on Sunday. You always mean to.

The stronger one puts the reader in a moment they know and sets up the subscription as the fix without saying it yet.

**Facts only the writer has:** the real situations and phrases customers use when they describe the problem, taken from reviews, calls or surveys.
