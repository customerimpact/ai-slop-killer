# Problem

**Its job:** describe the reader's problem so accurately that they think "that's me", and want it solved.

**Needed on:** long sales pages and VSL or webinar scripts, after the opening. Landing pages need a short version of it. Marketing emails and ads often lead with it. Product pages can keep it to a line or two. Skip or shrink it for readers who already know the product well and just want to buy, such as on order pages or offers to existing customers.

## What good looks like
- It uses the reader's own words, the ones they'd type into a search bar or say to a friend.
- It's concrete: specific moments, symptoms and consequences, not abstract labels.
- It shows the problem from the inside (how it feels on a Tuesday), not only from the outside (an industry statistic).
- It names the problem the product actually solves, not a bigger one it can't.
- It's the right length for the reader's awareness. Readers who already feel the pain need a quick nod, not a lecture.
- It treats the reader with respect. The problem is common and fixable, not their fault.

## What usually goes wrong
- It's generic and could fit any product in the category.
- It's about the company's view ("Businesses face inefficiencies") instead of the reader's day.
- It piles on pain for too long, so it feels manipulative.
- It describes a problem the product doesn't really fix.
- It blames or talks down to the reader.
- It's missing, so the solution arrives before the reader cares.

## Scoring (0–3)
- **0:** missing where it's needed, or it actively hurts (insulting, exaggerated, wrong problem).
- **1:** present but weak: abstract, generic or written in company language.
- **2:** does its job: a recognisable problem in the reader's words, sized right, tied to what the product fixes.
- **3:** a reason to buy on its own: so precise the reader feels understood, and they're already looking for the fix before you offer it.

## Fixes to suggest
- Pull exact phrases from reviews, support tickets or sales calls and use them.
- Replace each abstract word with a scene: what happens, when, and what it costs that day.
- Cut any pain the product doesn't solve.
- Shorten it if the audience already knows the problem well.
- Remove anything that makes the reader feel stupid for having the problem.

## Example
Weak (Clay Weekends):
> Many people want to be creative but struggle to find the right outlet for their artistic side.

Stronger:
> You've watched the videos. You bought the clay. Then the first bowl collapsed on the wheel, and the bag has been in the cupboard since March.

The stronger one describes a moment the beginner has lived, which makes the course feel written for them.

**Facts only the writer has:** the real reasons students give for quitting before, from surveys, comments or sign-up questions.
