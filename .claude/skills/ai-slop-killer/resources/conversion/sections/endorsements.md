# Endorsements

**Its job:** borrow trust from a recognised expert, institution or partner the reader already respects, so the product inherits some of that credibility.

**Needed on:** sales pages, landing pages and product pages where the reader looks to authority (health, finance, education, B2B software with integrations or certifications). Place near the claim the endorser can vouch for, or near the credibility block. Ads can carry one endorsement if it's well known to the audience. Skip it when there's no real endorser. Never imply one.

## What good looks like
- The endorser is relevant: their expertise matches the claim they're backing.
- They're recognised by this audience, not just famous in general.
- The endorsement says something specific about the product, ideally in their own words.
- The relationship is clear: partner, certifying body, paid advisor, independent reviewer. Paid ties are disclosed.
- There's a way to check: a link, a certificate number, a partner listing.
- It's one or two strong endorsements, not a crowd of loose name-drops.

## What usually goes wrong
- Names used without a real endorsement (they spoke at the same event, or wrote on the topic once).
- Endorsers with no link to the claim, like a sports star backing accounting software.
- "Recommended by experts" with no names.
- Logos of platforms the product merely integrates with, shown as if they endorse it.
- Paid endorsements presented as independent.
- Quotes so generic they could be about any product.

## Scoring (0–3)
- **0:** missing where it's needed, or it actively hurts (for example misleading or implied endorsements).
- **1:** present but weak: vague, loosely connected or hard to verify.
- **2:** does its job: a relevant, recognised endorser saying something specific, with the relationship clear.
- **3:** a reason to buy on its own: the endorsement removes the reader's biggest doubt, from someone they already trust.

## Fixes to suggest
- Keep only endorsers whose expertise matches a claim on the page. Put each next to that claim.
- Ask the endorser for one specific sentence about what they checked or saw.
- State the relationship in plain words, including any payment.
- Label integrations and certifications as what they are ("Works with", "Certified by").
- Add a link or reference number so the reader can verify.

## Example
**Weak (Ledgerly):**
> Recommended by top accountants and trusted by leading banks!

**Stronger (Ledgerly):**
> "I checked Ledgerly's VAT reports against twenty of my freelance clients' returns. They matched every time." Accountant who advises freelancers, unpaid reviewer.
> Bank feeds connect through licensed open-banking providers (listed on our security page).

Why it works: a relevant expert says what they actually checked, and the bank claim is stated as what it is.

**Facts only the writer has:** endorser names, their exact words and permission, the relationship and any payment, certification numbers, partner links.
