# Subheadline

**Its job:** finish what the headline started, by saying what this is, who it's for or how the promise happens, so the reader keeps going.

**Needed on:** sales pages, landing pages, product pages and opt-in pages, directly under the headline. In an ad it's the line after the hook or the description field. In an email the preview text plays this role. A webinar or VSL script covers it in the second and third sentences. Skip it only when the headline already says what the thing is and who it's for, and a second line would repeat it.

## What good looks like
- It adds new information: the mechanism, the product type, the audience, or the proof behind the headline.
- It makes the headline more believable, not louder.
- It's one or two sentences. The reader takes it in at a glance.
- After reading headline and subheadline together, a stranger could say what's being sold.
- It removes the most likely doubt the headline creates ("for beginners?", "how long?", "what does it cost me in effort?").

## What usually goes wrong
- It repeats the headline in different words.
- It's a paragraph of features stacked with commas.
- It's vague filler ("The tool you've been waiting for").
- It introduces a second, competing promise.
- It's missing, and the reader has to scroll to find out what the product even is.
- It's written for search engines or the brand team, not for the reader.

## Scoring (0–3)
- **0:** missing where it's needed, or it actively hurts.
- **1:** present but weak: repeats the headline, is generic, or is a crowded list of features.
- **2:** does its job: adds the what, who or how, and leaves no doubt about what's on offer.
- **3:** a reason to buy on its own: it makes the headline credible and answers the reader's first objection in one clean line.

## Fixes to suggest
- Ask what a sceptical reader would say right after the headline, and answer that in the subheadline.
- Name the product type and the reader plainly, if the headline didn't.
- Add the "how" in a few words: the method, the format or the time commitment.
- Cut it to the single most useful sentence.
- Make sure it doesn't make a new, bigger promise than the headline.

## Example
Weak (Ines Strength):
> The ultimate program for a healthier, stronger, better you.

Stronger:
> A 12-week plan built for people who sit all day: three 40-minute sessions a week at home or the gym, with Ines checking your form by video.

The stronger one says who it's for, what it involves and why it's different, so the headline's promise has something behind it.

**Facts only the writer has:** session length and frequency, equipment needed, and exactly how coaching feedback is delivered.
